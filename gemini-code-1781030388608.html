What To Do After a Car Accident: The Step-by-Step Guide

Clear, neutral, medical and legal-first checklist to protect your health and your rights after a vehicle crash in the United States.

Read the Checklist

Critical Action Protocol

1

Stop & Ensure Safety Immediately

Never drive away from an accident scene, even a minor one (leaving can be classified as a hit-and-run). Turn on your hazard lights. If possible and safe, move vehicles out of active traffic to the shoulder. Turn off the ignition to prevent fire risks.

2

Check for Injuries & Call 911

Check yourself, your passengers, and occupants of other vehicles for visible injuries or shock. Call 911 immediately to dispatch police and emergency medical services (EMS). Clearly state your location, the number of vehicles involved, and any immediate medical distress.

3

Exchange Essential Information Only

Exchange details with the other driver(s): Full name, contact info, insurance company, policy number, driver's license number, and license plate. Crucial: Do not apologize, argue, or discuss fault. Statements like "I'm sorry" can be legally used against you later to assign liability.

4

Document the Scene Thoroughly

Use your smartphone to capture high-resolution photos and videos: vehicle damage (close-up and wide shots), license plates, road conditions, traffic signs, skid marks, and weather. Note down the names and phone numbers of any independent eyewitnesses before they leave.

5

Obtain the Official Police Report

When officers arrive, answer their questions truthfully but objectively. Ask the responding officer for their name, badge number, and the Police Report Receipt Number. This official documentation is critical for insurance claims and legal recovery.

6

Seek Medical Care Within 72 Hours

Adrenaline masked pain. Conditions like whiplash, internal bleeding, or concussions might not show severe symptoms for hours or days. Seeking immediate care at an ER or Urgent Care creates an official medical timeline proving the accident caused the injuries.

⚠️ 3 Critical Mistakes That Ruin Claims

  • ✕ Accepting Early Settlements: Insurance companies frequently offer fast cash payouts right after a crash. Accepting prevents you from seeking further compensation if your medical injuries end up requiring long-term care or surgery.
  • ✕ Giving Recorded Statements: Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance adjuster without consulting an attorney. Adjusters use strategic phrasing to trick you into admitting partial or full fault.
  • ✕ Posting on Social Media: Avoid uploading photos of the crash, talking about your injuries, or claiming you are "feeling fine" on Facebook, Instagram, or TikTok. Defense adjusters actively monitor public profiles to devalue your injury claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if the other driver doesn't have insurance?

If you are hit by an uninsured or underinsured motorist, your own insurance policy's UM/UIM coverage typically covers your medical bills and property damages, depending on your state regulations.

Do I have to report a minor scratch to the police?

Most states legally require reporting any accident involving injuries or property damage exceeding a specific financial threshold (usually $500 to $1,000). It is always safest to file a report.
